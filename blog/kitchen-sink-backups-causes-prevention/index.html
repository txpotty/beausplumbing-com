    Kitchen Sink Backups: Causes, Prevention and Quick Solutions

    By Dana Whitfield · September 9, 2026 · 5 min read

    The kitchen drain takes more abuse than any other line in the house. Food scraps, cooking oil, coffee grounds and dishwasher discharge go down it every day. Nobody notices until water stands in the basin. Kitchen backups are one of the most common calls we run in Louisiana, and nearly all of them trace to the same few causes.

    What Causes Kitchen Sink Backups

    Grease is the main culprit. Bacon fat and pan drippings go down hot and liquid, then set up like candle wax once they hit the cool trap and branch line. That layer grabs food particles and builds into a thick, sticky plug. Where the water is hard, calcium scale and soap residue bond with the grease into a crust that hot water alone will not touch.

    Some Louisiana groundwater carries iron and sulfur, which can cause sulfur smells, stains, and buildup in water heaters.

    Garbage disposals come next. The disposal chops food up, but every bit of it still has to travel down the pipe. Fibrous stuff like celery, corn husks, artichoke leaves and onion skins wraps around the grinding plate and mats up in the drain. Pasta and potato peels swell into a paste. Coffee grounds and eggshells never dissolve and settle in low spots like sand.

    Some causes are harder to spot. Old cast iron and galvanized drain lines rust rough on the inside, and every rough patch is a place for grease to grab. A clogged dishwasher drain hose, or one installed without a high loop, can push water back into the sink. When the roof vent is plugged with leaves or a bird nest, the drain slows down and gurgles. If both sides of a double sink back up together, the clog is past the tee where they join.

    Quick Fixes You Can Try

    Start with the simple stuff before anything stronger. On a slow or standing sink, these steps clear most clogs:

    • Check the disposal. Kill the power, then use the 1/4 inch hex key in the bottom of the unit to free a jam and push the red reset button on the underside.
    • Plunge with the other side sealed. On a double sink, plug the second drain with a stopper or wet rag so the plunger pressure goes at the clog.
    • Pull the P-trap. Set a bucket underneath, loosen the two slip nuts and clear the trap by hand.
    • Run a hand auger. A small drum auger reaches a few feet into the wall to break up a grease plug.
    • Flush with hot water and dish soap once the clog is open, to push loosened grease further down the line.

    Skip the chemical drain openers. The lye and acid in those bottles generate real heat, enough to soften PVC and damage disposal seals. They also make the drain dangerous for whoever opens it next. When the trap is clean and the plunger did nothing, the blockage is probably in the wall, past where a hand auger reaches. That takes a longer cable.

    How to Prevent the Next Backup

    Prevention is about what goes down the drain. Pour cooled grease into a can and put it in the trash. Wipe greasy pans with a paper towel before they go in the sink. Scrape plates into the trash or compost and use the disposal only for small, soft scraps with cold water running. Run cold, not hot, so fats stay solid, get chopped and move on instead of melting and coating the pipe downstream.

    Once a month, fill the sink with hot water and pull the stopper. The full basin draining at once scours the trap. A water softener helps too, since less scale means smoother drain walls. Houses with repeat backups usually do better on a professional cleaning every year or two, especially when the kitchen line is long or runs into an old cast iron stack.

    When to Call a Professional

    Some signs mean the problem is deeper than the trap. The sink backs up again within days, water comes up in another fixture, or the dishwasher gurgles. Those point to a branch or main line problem.
